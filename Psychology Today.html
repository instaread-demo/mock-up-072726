Why Conversations Leave Us Emotionally Exhausted

Being present means staying out of someone else’s story.

Posted July 25, 2026 | Reviewed by Kaja Perina

When we think about being present, we usually picture putting our phones away, slowing down, or practicing mindfulness. We imagine learning to quiet our thoughts and focus on the moment. But there’s another kind of presence that rarely gets discussed. It’s the kind that happens in our conversations. And it may be one of the overlooked reasons so many helpers, parents, leaders, and partners end conversations feeling emotionally drained.

Our Brains Naturally Form Associations During Conversations

Picture this: Someone begins sharing something difficult. Before they’ve even finished their sentence, your brain has already gotten to work.

"That happened to me once."

"I know exactly what they should do."

"I should tell them what worked for me."

"This reminds me of..."

This isn’t because we’re selfish or poor listeners. It’s because our brains are designed to make connections. New information naturally connects to past experiences, helping us make sense of the world through memory and meaning.

From a neuroscience perspective, this process is exactly how our brains help us understand and navigate the world. Research on memory and cognition shows that our experiences are not stored as isolated pieces of information. Instead, memories are organized through interconnected networks, meaning that encountering something new can automatically activate related memories, emotions, and past experiences. This process, often referred to as associative memory, allows us to quickly interpret information and find meaning in what we are experiencing.

Source: Pavel Danilyuk/Pexels

When someone shares something difficult with us, our brain naturally searches for connections. It may retrieve a similar situation we experienced, a lesson we learned, or a solution that helped us in the past.

These associations are not a problem. In many ways, they are part of what allows us to relate to and empathize with others. The challenge is that we often mistake the first association our brain offers for the response the other person needs from us. Too often, we begin speaking from the association instead of continuing to listen from the present moment.

Without realizing it, the conversation shifts. We move from curiosity to commentary or from witnessing someone’s experience to comparing it with our own. We don’t simply notice these associations. We engage with them. We treat them as something that needs to be acted on, and before we realize it, we’ve projected our internal experience into the conversation. We haven’t left the conversation physically, but mentally we’ve stepped into our own story. That subtle shift changes everything.

The Difference Between Relating and Listening

As a therapist, I often see people mistake relating for listening. We assume that sharing our own experience communicates empathy. Sometimes it does, but timing is everything. When we relate too quickly, we unintentionally redirect the conversation before the other person has fully shared what they’re carrying.

Validation creates space. It doesn’t require us to silence our own thoughts. It asks us to acknowledge them, press pause, and stay present with the message and emotion the other person is sharing. This is the crossroads between supporting and solving. It’s the difference between saying, “Tell me more about what that’s been like for you,” and, “I know exactly how you feel. When that happened to me…”

The intention may be the same, but the impact is very different. One of the core ideas in my Support, Don’t Solve framework is that support doesn’t require becoming part of someone else’s story. It requires staying with their story long enough for them to better understand it themselves. By creating space instead of filling it, we give people the opportunity to process, reflect, and discover what they truly need. That can be surprisingly uncomfortable. Sometimes our urge to help isn’t only about the other person. It’s also about us.

Watching someone struggle can bring up our own discomfort. We want to make things better. We want to feel useful. We want reassurance that we’re being a good parent, leader, partner, or friend. Offering advice gives us something to do. Sharing our own experience can make us feel connected, but it can also reassure us that we’re useful, competent, and needed. But occasionally, what looks like compassion is actually an attempt to regulate our own discomfort.

We interrupt with solutions before fully understanding the problem. We reassure before we’ve validated because reassurance relieves our discomfort faster than validation relieves theirs. We move people toward our timeline instead of allowing them to move through their own. Helping becomes something we do to relieve our discomfort rather than something we offer for theirs.

The irony is that genuine presence often feels less productive in the moment. It asks us to tolerate uncertainty and resist the urge to fill the silence. It asks us to trust that understanding is often more valuable than immediate advice. Active listening doesn’t mean you won’t have thoughts, memories, or ideas. It means you notice them without allowing them to take over the conversation.

How to Practice Genuine Presence in Conversations

There are always two conversations happening. One is happening in front of you. The other is happening inside your own mind. Presence is recognizing the second conversation without allowing it to replace the first. When we do this, something important happens. The other person feels heard rather than managed. They feel understood rather than redirected. And perhaps just as importantly, we stop taking ownership of experiences that were never ours to carry. Many of us leave difficult conversations emotionally exhausted because we quietly inserted ourselves into them. We replay what we should have said, wonder whether we gave the right advice, or feel responsible for outcomes that were never ours to control.

Presence keeps emotional ownership with its rightful owner.

We can care deeply without carrying someone else’s burden.

This takes practice, but you’ll have plenty of opportunities to practice. The next time someone shares something vulnerable, notice what happens inside your own mind. You’ll probably have a memory, an opinion, advice, or a story. Let those thoughts come. They’re doing exactly what brains do. Then let them wait. Stay with the other person just a little longer. Presence isn’t measured by how quickly you respond. It’s measured by how long you’re willing to stay in someone else’s world before inviting them into yours.
